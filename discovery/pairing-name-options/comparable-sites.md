# Comparable sites and newsrooms: how they name the connection between money and what officials say or do, and how readers take it

**Kind of hop: a survey** of outside sources, written as findings. Each source below was read in full unless marked as seen only as a search snippet. No source's own reader research on these words was found. Summary: `findings.md`; the candidate words themselves: `name-options.md`.

## What was found, in one paragraph

No comparable site gives one noun to "what an official said, what a donor wants, and the money between them". They keep the parts in separate labelled sections and let the page, not a name, carry the connection (Digital Democracy). Where a word does join money to behaviour it is usually a causal or colourful one: "influence", "bankrolling", "backed by". The neutral words are the filing's own: contributions, contributors, donors. Organisations that do correlate money and votes (MapLight) add a statement that they do not claim cause, and have been criticised for blurring it anyway. Reader comments on a neutral data article show readers supplying the cause and the side themselves. A convention exists for the money word; none exists for the unit, so a card needs its own word with a short explanation. For spending by committees that do not give to the official, San Francisco sources use three different words (section 8). Research on corrections gives a reason to doubt a card line that names the idea it denies (section 9), by analogy only.

## 1. Digital Democracy (CalMatters), California Legislature

Read: an organisation page (https://calmatters.digitaldemocracy.org/organizations/-11770) and the Data & Methodology page (https://calmatters.digitaldemocracy.org/data-sources-methodology).

- **Sections, not a joined noun.** An organisation page has separate sections named Lobbying Representations, Bill Alignment, Financials and Testimony. "Bill Alignment" is a person's or organisation's recorded position on a bill ("Support", "Against"), not the match between a donor and a legislator. "Financials" is described as financial exchanges between an organisation and receivers, including campaign donations, gifts, independent expenditures and travel.
- **"Influence" is the site's own name for gifts, sponsored travel and behested payments to an incumbent legislator**, kept apart from "election money" (campaign contributions). The methodology says so in as many words. So the one site with money, speech transcripts and positions on one page uses the causal word for one money category and a neutral one for the other.
- **"Related Organizations".** The methodology and page explain that spellings and affiliates are linked by people and software, and that the database "will always be imprecise". That is the many-to-one donor problem the cards already describe, and the site explains it where the grouping is shown, not in a name.
- The page footer calls the site an oversight tool over "financial backers" of elected leaders, and the methodology calls its aim to compare data so that "relationships, patterns and aberrations" show. Both are causal-leaning words in the site's own description.

## 2. San Francisco Ethics Commission campaign dashboards

Read: https://campaign.sfethics.org/

- Its headline asks the reader to see who is "funding" San Francisco political campaigns, and its sub-line says the site explores "the money influencing" elections.
- Its contents are named neutrally: largest contributors, how much campaigns raise and spend, third-party activity and who funds it. "Contributions", "contributors", "third-party" and "spending" are the filing words.
- So the city's own site uses "funding" and "influencing" in its pitch and filing words in its data. A card that follows the data words and avoids the pitch words is consistent with the city's data even where it differs from the city's headline.

## 3. San Francisco newsrooms

Read: Mission Local, "Who's bankrolling San Francisco's coming election? These 16 donors." (https://missionlocal.org/2026/09/san-francisco-november-election-top-donors/); Mission Local, three incumbents re-elected "backed by similar allies" (https://missionlocal.org/2026/11/three-incumbent-sf-supervisors-win-reelection/, snippet); San Francisco Chronicle, "These are the wealthy people trying to reshape S.F. politics" (https://www.sfchronicle.com/projects/2026/san-francisco-election-donors/, snippet); KQED, "Big Money Against Progressive Incumbents" (https://www.kqed.org/news/12012416/san-franciscos-elections-big-money-against-progressive-incumbents, snippet).

- **Headlines carry colour; bodies and methodology notes are plain.** The Mission Local article's headline says "bankrolling"; its body says "donors", "contributed" and "raised", and its methodology states the data source, date and grouping rule (donors grouped with spouse or companies).
- **Verbs of force.** The other three use "trying to reshape", "poured" and "backed by similar allies". "Backed by" in the Mission Local article blends donors with endorsers (the article's own text lists party and group endorsements next to fundraising advantages), the confusion the cards avoid by showing only filed money.
- **Political-camp labels in text.** "Moderate", "progressive", "tech-backed" and "pro-housing" appear as descriptors of groups. A card that used them would take a side in San Francisco's own vocabulary.
- Every one of these pieces describes money and politics in the newsroom's voice. None shows the quote and the donor's own words together.

## 4. How readers took one of these (reader comments)

Read: the twelve comments under the Mission Local donors article (same URL as above).

- At least four of the twelve comments supply a cause or a villain the article itself did not claim (one calls the list "corruption" and "oligarchy"; one says the measures hand power to an "elite ruling class"). One calls the list "basically your voting guide" and says to want the opposite of what the donors want. One defends donors' right to give. The rest are about the measures.
- This is twelve comments on one article and says nothing about readers in general. It does show readers sorting donors into sides and into causes from a donor list, even though the article's own body is neutral.
- **A related empirical result (snippet only; the full paper could not be opened, so beyond the abstract it is unverified).** A law-school experiment with grand-jury simulations (Robertson, Winkelman, Bergstrand and Modzelewski, "The Appearance and the Reality of Quid Pro Quo Corruption", https://scholarship.law.bu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1964&context=faculty_scholarship) reports that participants found quid pro quo corruption when spending was connected to policy, nearly as often as for outright bribery. If that holds, putting money next to an official's words invites the reading by itself; no choice of name removes it, and the explanation and the order of the card carry more weight than the name.

## 5. MapLight (money and votes)

Read: a Stanford Social Innovation Review profile (https://ssir.org/articles/entry/follow_the_money, search snippet) and a 2008 post by GovTrack's founder (https://joshdata.wordpress.com/2008/03/07/money-is-not-quite-so-big-of-an-incentive-for-voting-with-your-wallet/, search snippet).

- MapLight joins contributions to how legislators vote and to which groups support or oppose a bill. The profile says plainly that correlation is not causation.
- MapLight's director, quoted in the post, says its analysis does not claim one thing caused the other, while saying contributions bias the legislative system. The site both disclaims cause and takes a position on how money and politics should be run (the encyclopedia entry in the same search, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MapLight, snippet, records its disagreement with Supreme Court campaign-finance rulings). A disclaimer sitting beside advocacy is the combination the Vision's second non-goal rules out for this site.
- GovTrack's founder criticised the analysis for blurring correlation and causation, and added a partisan point: contributions follow party, and so do votes, so a correlation between money and votes appears even if no donor considered the vote.
- **MapLight today.** Its current About page (https://www.maplight.org/about-us) describes it as a nonprofit, nonpartisan maker of software for campaign finance, lobbying and ethics disclosure that governments buy; the money-and-votes database of the older pages is not described there. Its Voter's Edge page (https://www.maplight.org/votersedge) says the decade-long partnership with the League of Women Voters of California Education Fund has ended and sends readers to VOTE411.
- Lesson for the cards, from a source that was read: the confound a critic named is party, so the pairing's own words ("same subject", "different positions") are doing the work a party control would do; a card cannot show that a donor's gift was not decided by party.

## 6. ProPublica "Dollars for Docs" (payments to physicians)

Read: three article pages as search snippets (for example https://www.propublica.org/article/we-found-over-700-doctors-who-were-paid-more-than-a-million-dollars-by-drug-and-medical-device-companies and https://www.propublica.org/article/with-our-dollars-for-docs-update-coming-drug-companies-defend-interactions).

- The database is named for the money ("Dollars"), and the individual items are "payments", which is accurate there because the doctors were paid for talks and consulting. ProPublica's pieces separate the data (payments) from published studies that matched payments to prescribing; the snippet read cuts off at the studies' result, so how ProPublica words the result was not read.
- An industry trade group's own release is described as calling paid speaking "peer education programs" and the payments "interactions", a point ProPublica makes against it. That is an example of the opposite problem: a euphemism that hides what the data shows. A neutral word for a card should not be one the donors would pick to hide the money.

## 7. Other sources

- **Pay-to-play as a legal term.** Law-firm material on the SEC's investment-adviser rule (https://natlawreview.com/article/pay-to-play-regulation-and-enforcement-2022) describes "pay to play" as a rule that bars an adviser from being paid for government work for two years after certain political contributions. In law the phrase means money in exchange for a contract, so using it of a donor and a speech would claim an exchange.
- **A research summary** from an advocacy group (https://americasplan.org/issues/campaign-finance-policy-outcomes/, snippet; its own funding and viewpoint were not checked, so treat it as unverified) states that an observed correlation between contributions and votes may reflect the money causing the vote or an alignment that existed before any money changed hands. This is the same point as the GovTrack critique, made from the other direction.

## 8. Words for spending that is not given to the official's committees (Form 496)

Read: the SF Ethics Commission's list of forms (https://sfethics.org/guidance/campaigns/complete-list-of-campaign-public-finance-forms), its page on expenditure ceilings and third-party spending (https://sfethics.org/disclosures/campaign-finance-disclosure/expenditure-ceilings-and-third-party-spending, search snippet) and its contributor reporting page (https://sfethics.org/guidance/campaigns/contributors/contributor-third-party-reporting-requirements, search snippet); Mission Local, "Meet the District 6 candidates: Should S.F. curb the influence of big spending in elections?" (https://missionlocal.org/2026/10/meet-the-district-6-candidates-should-sf-curb-influence-unlimited-outside-spending-elections/) and "Money in San Francisco elections is growing. City watchdogs are getting cut." (https://missionlocal.org/2026/07/big-money-in-san-francisco-elections-is-growing-but-city-watchdogs-are-getting-cut/, snippet); a Statista chart crediting OpenSecrets (https://statista.com/statistics/611303/outside-spending-in-us-political-cycles, snippet).

- **The filing's own name is "independent expenditure".** The Ethics Commission lists FPPC Form 496 as the "Independent Expenditure Report" and describes the 24-hour report as one filed when independent expenditures of $1,000 or more are made in connection with a candidate or measure.
- **The Ethics Commission's own umbrella word is "third-party spending".** Its page heading is "Expenditure Ceilings and Third-Party Spending", and the page says independent expenditures, electioneering communications and member communications are the disclosed kinds.
- **Mission Local uses more than one word.** In the October 2026 article the reporter's own text says "outside spending", "political committees, or PACs", "independent political committees" and "independent committees"; a candidate's quoted answer there says "independent expenditure committees" and "outside groups". The July 2026 article uses "third-party spending" with figures from its own analysis. So a San Francisco newsroom does use "outside spending", alongside the other words; none of the pages read calls any one of them the usual term.
- **"Outside spending" in the federal setting.** The Statista chart's definition, credited to OpenSecrets, describes it as political spending by groups independent of candidate committees (PACs, super PACs, 527 and 501(c) groups). The page read is a chart from a third party, not an OpenSecrets page.
- **What this means for the card.** The money zone's separate line can carry the filing's word ("independent expenditures, Form 496") with "outside spending" or "third-party spending" as the plain-words gloss. Which gloss to use is open; the maintainer's Project Memory already says "Outside spending is kept apart and labelled separately".

## 9. Research on how readers take a denial ("no cause is claimed")

Read: Lewandowsky, Ecker, Seifert, Schwarz and Cook, "Misinformation and its Correction: Continued Influence and Successful Debiasing", Psychological Science in the Public Interest (2012), in the authors' in-press manuscript (https://skepticalscience.com/docs/Lewandowsky_2012_misinfo.pdf).

- **Repeating a claim to deny it can raise its familiarity.** The review says retractions may fail or backfire when they directly or indirectly repeat the false information, which makes it more familiar. It reports a study in which strengthening a denial ("paint and gas were never on the premises") made readers more likely to use the claim, and a "myth versus fact" handout study in which readers, after about 30 minutes, labelled more myths as facts than people who had not seen the handout.
- **The authors' advice is to emphasise the facts, not the myth,** and to give a warning before the myth is mentioned. They add that warnings work when they explain how misinformation goes on influencing people, not when they only say it may be present.
- **Limits of this evidence for the card.** The studies concern claims people have already taken in and are then told were wrong, mostly in laboratory reading tasks with invented events. A card line that says "no cause is claimed" is not a retraction of something the reader was told. No study of a disclaimer on a display that sets donations beside a politician's words was found. The review is from 2012; later work on backfire effects was not read. Applying it to the card is by analogy (my reading): it is a reason to prefer a sentence that says what the card shows, and a reason to test both wordings (`reader-check.md`).
- **Related result in `comparable-sites.md` section 5:** MapLight's disclaimer of cause sat beside advocacy, and critics still read it as blurring the point. That is a separate matter from familiarity.

## 10. Voter's Edge and the League of Women Voters of California

Read: MapLight's launch post for Voter's Edge California (https://www.maplight.org/post/voter-s-edge-california-launches-to-guide-voters-and-fight-misinformation) and, as a search snippet, the LWVC post on its embeddable cards (https://lwvc.org/voter-s-edge-california-offers-ballot-measure-and-candidate-cards-for-media-outlets-and-nonprofit-websites/). The Voter's Edge site itself (votersedge.org) was not read.

- **Separate labelled parts, no joined noun.** The guide lists candidates' "priorities, endorsements and top donors", and for ballot measures "supporters and opponents" with "campaign funding" for the "No" and "Yes" campaigns. The unit it publishes is called a "card" (a "candidate card" or "ballot measure card"), and the cards carry "top donors and endorsements".
- **Neutral money words, a plain "card" for the unit.** "Top donors", "campaign funding" and "funding sources" are the money words; the donor and the candidate's priorities are in different parts. Nothing on the pages read ties a donor to a candidate's stated priority.
- **Self-description.** The post calls the guide "nonpartisan" and "unbiased" and reports reader surveys by the publisher (more than 90 percent of users said it made them feel more confident), which say nothing about how readers take any single word.
- **The product has ended.** MapLight's current Voter's Edge page says the partnership has ended (section 5), so its wording is a record of what California voters were shown, not a live convention.

How OpenSecrets labels the connection between donors and what officials say is not known from any source read.

## What this means for the charter question on convention

A convention exists for the money word (contributions, contributors, donors) and for the practice of keeping the sections apart (Digital Democracy, Voter's Edge). For Form 496 spending there are three words in use in San Francisco (independent expenditure, third-party spending, outside spending), and none is the single usual one. None exists for a single word for the three-part unit. The nearest word used in this way, "alignment", describes a position on a bill and implies agreement. A card therefore either keeps its own invented word with its explanation, as "pairing" does now, or uses descriptive words and no unit noun.
