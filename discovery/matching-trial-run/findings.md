# Matching trial run: which ways of pairing a quote with a donor priority stand out from chance

**Kind of hop: a survey with a measurement.** It surveys real sources outside the project (caption transcripts of San Francisco Board of Supervisors meetings, and donor priority statements on donors' own sites) and then runs five ways of comparing a quote with a priority over them, counting what each finds, what a random-word model finds at the same settings, and what a reader makes of a sample. It makes no decisions; where it says what the numbers "suggest" that is a reading for the next Hop. Everything computed here is regenerated by `python analyze.py` from the snapshot in `data/` (`results.md`, `results.json`, `label_sample.json`); `collect.py` re-takes the snapshot.

Builds on `../speech-sources-survey/findings.md` (transcripts and their stamps), `../quote-attribution-check/findings.md` (speaker is not in the caption text), `../donor-priorities-survey/findings.md` (which donors publish priorities) and `../campaign-finance-survey/findings.md` (amounts, not joined here).

## Summary by charter question

1. **How many candidate pairings does each way find, and how many would turn up by chance?** On 3,065 substantive passages from 11 Board and committee meetings (January to July 2026) against 133 priority statements from six donors (407,645 pairs per way):

   | Way | Loosest setting: real / chance (random words, pooled) | Setting where chance falls under one pair per run: real / chance |
   |---|---|---|
   | TF-IDF cosine | 472 / 55.5 (cosine 0.15) | 32 / 0.1 (cosine 0.25) |
   | Shared two-word phrase | 2,424 / 256.9 (one phrase) | 193 / 0.3 (two phrases) |
   | Shared topic (13-topic keyword list) | 1,752 / 343.6 (one topic; 806 on the within-meeting model) | 1 / 0.1 (two topics) |
   | Shared named thing (measure letter, name, acronym) | 1,219 / 848.9 (one anchor) | 66 / 0.0 (three anchors) |
   | Cosine and a shared phrase together | 208 / 1.9 (cosine 0.15) | 74 / 0.1 (cosine 0.20) |

   Two chance models were used (words redrawn from all quotes, or from the quote's own meeting; 20 draws each, `results.md` gives both with maxima). They agree to within a factor of about two. **Chance is a floor on noise, not an estimate of it:** it removes only pairs that need no real shared content. At the loosest settings chance is 12% of TF-IDF candidates, 11% of phrase candidates, 20 to 46% of topic candidates and 70% of anchor candidates, but a reader judged most of the surviving pairs not meaningful anyway (question 2).
2. **Which ways surface pairings a reader finds meaningful?** One reader (the author of this trial, not independent) judged 95 pairs drawn at random from each way's two settings, plus the best-scoring pairs (rubric below). Share judged *meaningful* at the setting where chance falls under one pair per run (14 pairs each, 95% range): anchor 57% (33 to 79%), TF-IDF 43% (21 to 67%), phrase 43% (21 to 67%), both together 43% (21 to 67%); topic produced one pair, judged coincidental. At the loosest settings: TF-IDF 12%, phrase 0%, topic 0%, anchor 25%, both 12% (8 pairs each, ranges 0 to 59%). The ranges overlap, so **the data do not rank the four working ways; they do show that threshold matters far more than the choice of way**, that a topic list is no use alone, and that what a matching way mostly finds is a **named ballot measure or program** (the Housing Trust Fund and the real property transfer tax account for nearly all anchor matches). The pairs judged meaningful are real exchanges on the same measure: a supervisor explaining the Housing Trust Fund charter amendment against the Chamber's and GrowSF's positions on Prop C, supervisors discussing the foreclosure-exemption transfer tax measure against GrowSF's and Blueprint's positions on Prop J, a supervisor describing the City Administrator measure against the Chamber's position on Prop E.
3. **How many candidates would a nightly run produce, and how long would one person take?** From this snapshot, at the settings where chance is under one pair per run and with a weekly pattern of one Board meeting and three committee meetings: **about 10 (TF-IDF) to 64 (phrase) candidate pairs a week, 27 for cosine-plus-phrase, 23 for anchor**, against 133 statements. Transcripts arrive in a weekly batch, so most nights would offer none and a night after a Board meeting and a committee would offer 18 to 94 (busy-night figures, from the busiest July meetings). Reviewing the 27 a week of cosine-plus-phrase would take roughly **47 to 117 minutes a week** (an average night 7 to 17 minutes; a busy night 65 to 160 minutes); the range comes from an assumed 2 to 8 minutes to verify each pair a reader keeps. **Those times rest on assumptions, not on timing a person.** Candidates scale with the number of priority statements: at 1,000 statements the same rule gives about 204 a week (about 6 to 15 hours of review a week at the same rates), which one person running the site alone could not sustain without a cap.

**Read these figures with these limits.** The snapshot is July-heavy (9 of 11 meetings are 7 to 21 July 2026, when the Board was putting the November measures on the ballot), so weekly figures are probably higher than an average week and the proportion of measure-specific matches is probably higher than outside ballot season. Only six donors' pages are used (all organisations; no individual donor has a fetchable statement). The reader judgements are one person's, on 95 pairs.

## What was run

### Quotes

- **Transcripts.** The Legistar caption transcript (`Transcript.aspx?ID1=<n>`) of 11 meetings, fetched 29 October 2026: Board of Supervisors on 6 January, 21 April, 7, 14 and 21 July; Budget and Finance on 8 and 15 July; Public Safety and Neighborhood Services on 9 July; Rules and Land Use and Transportation on 13 July; Government Audit and Oversight on 16 July (24,454 caption rows; `data/meetings.json`, `data/transcripts/<id>.tsv`, one `HH:MM:SS<TAB>text` row per line). Transcript ids were found by probing ids near the ones the earlier surveys recorded; transcripts for many other city bodies share the same id space, and a duplicate id pair exists (52800 and 52801 are the same Board meeting; 52845 and 52849 likewise).
- **Passages.** A passage is one to three sentences: a turn ends at `>>` (the captions' only speaker-change mark), and a passage closes at the first sentence end after 45 words or at 90 words, keeping the stamp of its first word. 4,904 passages; 3,065 are "substantive" (at least 25 words, at least 12 distinct content words, fewer than two procedural phrases such as "roll call" or "madam clerk"). The caption text is in capitals and carries no speaker names, so **a passage may be an official, a clerk, a department head, an applicant or a member of the public.** Nothing in this trial identifies the speaker.

### Priorities

133 statements from six donors' own pages, fetched 29 October 2026 (`data/priorities.json`, each with donor, URL, section heading and page date where the page shows one): the Chamber of Commerce's legislative agenda (33 bullets) and November 2026 voter guide (16 measure paragraphs); GrowSF's four stated priorities and, for each November city measure A to J, the paragraphs under "Why vote" on the side GrowSF endorses (47; "Claim:" lines, which quote a measure's backers, were left out); Blueprint for a Better San Francisco (the Neighbors for a Better San Francisco project): three pillars, its "about" line and its ten measure recommendations (14); SEIU Local 1021's first page of issue articles (18); the San Francisco Police Officers Association mission line and two own-voice article teasers (3); Airbnb's 2019 newsroom post (2). A statement is at most 90 words, cut at a sentence end. Section headings are matched together with the text, because a GrowSF paragraph often names the measure only in its heading.

### Five ways of comparing a quote with a priority

| Way | What is compared | Score |
|---|---|---|
| `tfidf` | Stemmed content words, weighted by how rare they are in all quotes and priorities | Cosine, 0.15 to 0.50 |
| `phrase` | Pairs of adjacent content words, leaving out pairs that occur in more than 2% of quotes (such as "public safety") | Number of shared pairs, 1 to 3 |
| `topic` | Thirteen topics (housing, homelessness, public safety, drugs and health, transit and streets, budget and taxes, business and downtown, city government, schools, labor, climate and resilience, arts and culture, federal policy), each a hand-written keyword list; a text has a topic when it uses three different keywords | Number of shared topics |
| `anchor` | Named things taken from the priority: a measure ("Prop H"), capitalised names, acronyms, minus any that occur in more than 1% of quotes | Number of shared anchors, 1 to 3 |
| `both` | Cosine, counted only where a rare phrase is also shared | Cosine |

Not tried: embedding similarity or a language model's judgement of a pair (neither was a dependency this Hop could choose; a model judge would need its own key and a current model name). The ways above are a lexical ladder from loose to tight; a model judge is the obvious next rung and is untested here.

### Chance

Two random-word models, 20 draws each: **pooled** (every quote is replaced by one of the same length whose words are drawn, with their frequency, from all quotes) and **within meeting** (words drawn from the quote's own meeting, which keeps a meeting's vocabulary but no passage's sense). The same rule is then run against the same priorities. A way's **calibrated setting** is its loosest setting at which both models give a mean under one pair per run. This is a definition, not a recommendation. It does not model two kinds of look-alike that the readers' judgements show: a real passage and a real priority on the same broad subject, and the same name meaning two things.

### Reader's rubric

Pairs were drawn by a fixed hash (8 at each way's loosest setting, 14 at its calibrated setting, 4 best-scoring), 95 pairs in all (`label_sample.json`; judgements and one-line notes in `data/labels.json`):

- **meaningful**: the passage speaks to the same specific measure, program, proposal or stated position as the priority, so a reader would see why the two sit side by side (the speaker is not confirmed);
- **topical**: same broad subject only (both about the drug crisis, both about transit);
- **coincidental**: shared words or numbers, different subjects;
- **not a quote**: related, but the passage is a clerk reading a title or agenda, a public comment, an applicant for a board seat, or a department presentation.

Judgements are one reader's and a stance is not judged: a supervisor opposing the Housing Trust Fund measure pairs "meaningfully" with a donor supporting it.

## Results by way

Counts are real / chance mean. Full tables for every setting are in `results.md`.

### TF-IDF cosine

32 candidates at cosine 0.25 or more (chance 0.1 pooled, 0.5 within meeting), from 26 quotes and 26 priorities; 111 at 0.20 (chance 3.1 and 5.8); 472 at 0.15 (chance 55.5 and 75.2). Readers: at 0.25, 6 of 14 meaningful, 4 topical, 1 coincidental, 3 not a quote; at 0.15, 1 of 8 meaningful. The best-scoring four were two meaningful and two topical. It needs no hand-built list and finds the measure-specific pairs, but it also pairs a short priority with any passage that shares a few distinctive words ("Treatment and care for the drug crisis" against any drug remark).

### Shared two-word phrase

193 candidates at two or more shared rare phrases (chance 0.3), from 86 quotes and 57 priorities; 68 at three or more; 2,424 at one (chance 257 to 268). Readers: at two phrases, 6 of 14 meaningful, 3 topical, 3 coincidental, 2 not a quote; at one phrase, 0 of 8 meaningful (7 coincidental). Its four best-scoring pairs were all the **clerk reading the title of the Prop B charter amendment** against four different Prop B statements: a long official title shares many phrases with any text about the measure and is not a statement. It yields the most candidates (64 a week) and the most review.

### Shared topic

At three keywords per side, one topic shared: 1,752 candidates against 344 (pooled) to 806 (within meeting) by chance; two topics shared: one pair, judged coincidental. At the loosest setting 0 of 8 sampled pairs were meaningful (7 coincidental). The topic list as built is not usable alone: a topic is wide, and a random word draw reaches it often. (A narrower list was not tried.)

### Shared named thing

66 candidates with three or more shared anchors (chance 0.0), from 28 quotes and 18 priorities, 56% of them on three priorities; 89 at two (chance 1.0 and 0.3); 1,219 at one (chance about 849, 70%). Readers: at three anchors, 8 of 14 meaningful, 1 topical, 2 coincidental, 3 not a quote; at one anchor, 2 of 8. The shared anchors in the 66 are almost all "Housing Trust Fund" (32 pairs) and "real property transfer tax" (24); "civil grand jury" (5 pairs, judged coincidental) and "Laguna Honda Hospital" (2, coincidental) are the other recurring ones. **Measure letters did not carry it:** only 13 of the 3,065 substantive passages say "prop" followed by a letter or number, and several mean other years' measures (Prop M, Prop L, and a "Prop C" that was the early-education fund, paired by the match with the housing Prop C and judged coincidental). Spoken references in July name measures by what they do, not by the letter they were given later (my reading, from the 13 passages).

### Cosine and a shared phrase together

74 candidates at cosine 0.20 or more with a shared rare phrase (chance 0.1), from 48 quotes and 45 priorities; 208 at 0.15 (chance 1.9 to 2.6, the lowest chance share of any loose setting). Readers: at 0.20, 6 of 14 meaningful, 4 topical, 3 coincidental, 1 not a quote; at 0.15, 1 of 8 meaningful. Best-scoring four: three meaningful, one topical. Its chance count is the lowest at its loosest setting (1%), yet only 1 of 8 loose pairs was meaningful, which is the clearest case that a low chance count does not mean a reader finds the pair meaningful.

### Overlap

The ways find largely different pairs (Jaccard at the calibrated settings: TF-IDF with phrase 0.09, phrase with anchor 0.34, anchor with TF-IDF 0.09, both with TF-IDF 0.33). A pair found by two ways is not therefore better: the sample is too small to say.

## What the matches look like

- **What stood out.** Pairs about a specific named measure, in a sitting where it was being debated: the Housing Trust Fund charter amendment (7 and 14 July Board; 15 July Budget and Finance), the Municipal Finance Corporation and public bank (7 July), the City Administrator and executive-branch charter measures (14 July), the foreclosure exemption and transfer tax measures (15 July Budget and Finance), the ballot-measure process measure (13 July Rules), and the Great Highway ballot measure (6 January Board, when a supervisor raised it). One pair on a broad theme was judged meaningful because the wording was near-identical: a passage praising "back to basics" police services and "clean and safe" streets against GrowSF's "Clean, safe streets and fully staffed police".
- **What drowned it.**
  - *Clerk and agenda readings.* A clerk reading the title or digest of a measure matches every statement about that measure, strongly, and is not an official's statement (judged "not a quote": 15 of 95 pairs).
  - *Public comment, applicants and presentations.* They look like speech on the issue (recovery, supportive housing, downtown) and are not an official's words.
  - *Name collisions.* "Prop C", "Great Highway" (a housing project's address), "Civil Grand Jury", "Chamber of Commerce" (the Filipino one), "Laguna Honda Hospital".
  - *Broad priorities.* GrowSF's four themes and the Chamber's generic agenda lines attract passages about the subject without a shared position.
  - *Caption errors.* Names and numbers are garbled (the earlier surveys list examples), so a way that needs an exact name misses real mentions; none of the 95 judged pairs was decided by a caption error, but the sample was not built to test that.
- **Timing.** A priority about a November measure cannot be answered by a January or April quote except where the measure already existed (the Great Highway). Of the 74 cosine-plus-phrase candidates at the calibrated setting, 6 come from the January and April Board meetings and 68 from the nine July meetings (`results.json`, `per_meeting_candidates`).
- **Who is credited.** Every judged "meaningful" pair has an unconfirmed speaker. Where the text itself shows the speaker (a supervisor saying "my charter amendment"), the match is clear; for most passages, nothing in the text says who spoke.

## A nightly run: volume and review time

Assumptions, all stated in the script: a week brings one Board meeting and three committee meetings (about four meetings a week in the October 2026 calendar, from the speech survey); a night's run sees what has been posted since the last (transcripts appear within about three days, so batches follow meetings); a quote is paired with every priority, so a quote can produce several candidates.

| Way (calibrated setting) | Per Board meeting | Per committee meeting | Per week | Per night, average | Busy night | At 1,000 statements, per week |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TF-IDF 0.25 | 4.0 | 2.0 | 10.0 | 1.4 | 18 | 75 |
| Phrase, two | 22.0 | 13.8 | 63.5 | 9.1 | 94 | 477 |
| Anchor, three | 6.8 | 5.3 | 22.8 | 3.3 | 48 | 171 |
| Cosine 0.20 and phrase | 6.6 | 6.8 | 27.1 | 3.9 | 37 | 204 |
| Topic, two | 0.0 | 0.2 | 0.5 | 0.1 | 1 | 4 |

"Busy night" is the busiest sampled Board meeting plus the busiest sampled committee meeting landing together; the busiest meetings are the July measure-season ones. Pairs per quote at the calibrated settings are 1.2 (TF-IDF), 2.2 (phrase), 2.4 (anchor) and 1.5 (both), so grouping candidates by quote would shorten reading.

**Review time.** Triage reads both texts at 238 words a minute (Brysbaert's 2019 meta-analysis of silent reading of English non-fiction, a published figure) plus 15 seconds to decide: 42 to 49 seconds a pair for the four working ways. A pair the reader keeps is then verified: listening to the passage (150 words a minute, assumed; 21 to 25 seconds) plus a fixed 2, 4.5 or 8 minutes (assumed) to open the recording at the stamp, find the words, identify the speaker and open the donor's page. The share kept is the share judged meaningful at the calibrated setting (43% for TF-IDF, phrase and both, 57% for anchor; 14 pairs each, so rough). Week of review for one person:

| Way | Pairs a week | Minutes a week: fixed verify 2 / 4.5 / 8 min | Pairs reviewable in 30 minutes (8 / 4.5 / 2 min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| TF-IDF 0.25 | 10.0 | 17 / 28 / 43 | 7 / 11 / 18 |
| Phrase, two | 63.5 | 115 / 183 / 278 | 7 / 10 / 17 |
| Anchor, three | 22.8 | 50 / 82 / 128 | 5 / 8 / 14 |
| Cosine 0.20 and phrase | 27.1 | 47 / 76 / 117 | 7 / 11 / 17 |

The weekly minutes are computed from the table above with the rates in `results.json` (`review`); `results.md` gives average-night and busy-night minutes. A person who allows 30 minutes a night could verify about 5 to 18 candidates a night; the average night of the tighter ways is under that, a busy night is above it for every way. **No reviewer was timed**: the fixed verification times are assumptions, and the earlier surveys found that caption words cannot be trusted without listening, which is why verification is in the estimate.

## What the findings suggest for the objective

Reading, not decision:

- A key result for "only surface things that are meaningful" could be measured as *the share of candidates a reviewer keeps as meaningful*, with the random-word chance count as a floor and a reader-judged sample as the real test. This trial puts the first measurement at roughly 40 to 60% at the calibrated settings and under 25% at the loosest (small samples, one reader).
- "Stands out rather than chance" has two thresholds that need separating: (a) a chance threshold, where random words give under one pair per run (cosine about 0.25, two shared rare phrases, three shared anchors in this corpus; these move with corpus size and must be recomputed), and (b) a meaning threshold, where a reader keeps the pair. The first is cheap to compute each night; only the second needs people.
- Cheap filters that would have removed a visible share of the unmeaningful candidates here: dropping clerk title readings and agenda readings, dropping public-comment passages, and requiring the quote to be attributed to an official. None of them is available without the speaker (see `quote-attribution-check`).
- Volume is bounded by meetings, not by the nightly schedule: batches follow meetings, and the pool of statements scales it linearly.

## Open questions

**Looked for and not found**
- Any timing of a reviewer checking a pair. Looked at: the earlier surveys' findings and the repository; none holds one. The review times above are model output.
- An independent reader's judgement. The 95 judgements are one reader's; no second reader judged any pair, so agreement is not measured.
- Priorities for individual donors. The donor survey found one individual donor (of 16) with a class-A statement (a social-media profile), and no such page was fetched here; the 133 statements are all from organisations. Which of the 34 donors in the donor sample are covered: Chamber of Commerce, GrowSF, Neighbors for a Better San Francisco (Blueprint), SEIU Local 1021, the Police Officers Association and Airbnb; IFPTE Local 21's pages hold endorsements without reasons and were not used.
- Donor amounts. Dollar figures from the campaign finance survey were not joined to the pairs, so nothing here ranks pairings by money.
- Mayor speech. No Mayor press conference or other non-Board source was sampled.
- Any statement of the permitted fetch pace. `https://sfgov.legistar.com/robots.txt` returned HTTP 404 on 29 October 2026; the robots files and terms of the donor sites, Granicus and the Legistar service terms were not read.

**Claims not verified**
- That the 11 transcripts are complete: caption rows per meeting were counted (1,029 to 5,009), but no transcript was compared with its recording or with the meeting's duration.
- That the ten measures' sides in the GrowSF snapshot match its endorsement hub: the side was taken from the hub page's list at fetch time.
- That the 13 passages naming "prop" and a letter or number refer to the measures I took them to (my reading, from the surrounding words).
- That 15 of the 95 sampled pairs are "not a quote" because the speaker is a clerk, commenter, applicant or department staff: judged from the words alone, with no speaker identification.
- That measure-season July is unrepresentative of other weeks (my reading): the snapshot has no autumn or spring committee sample beyond one April Board meeting.
- The assumed speech rate of 150 words a minute and the three fixed verification times are assumptions, not findings.

**Questions for people**
- Maintainer: is a clerk's reading of a measure's title ever quotable as "what an official said"? It decides whether the largest source of look-alike pairs is dropped or shown.
- Maintainer: does a statement by a department head or a member of the public presenting at a hearing count as a politician's quote? The trial treated both as not a quote.
- Maintainer or a reporter: how many minutes a night is the site's operator willing to spend on review? Against the table above it fixes how many candidates a night the first version may offer.
- Two or three reporters or residents (for the key result): judge the 95 pairs in `label_sample.json` with the rubric above, and time a verification of ten kept pairs from stamp to citation. That would replace the assumed fixed times and test the single reader's rubric.
- Maintainer: should a priority from a donor's older page (Airbnb 2019) be paired with 2026 quotes at all? The statements here carry their page date; no age limit was applied.
